# Checklist for hiring a hardware design firm

What to settle before you hire an outside firm or contractor for electronics design: scope, deliverables, ownership, process, cost, and production.

Interactive and printable version: [https://westlakesys.com/tools/checklists/hiring-a-design-firm/](https://westlakesys.com/tools/checklists/hiring-a-design-firm/)

What to settle before you sign with an outside firm or contractor for electronics design, for founders and teams buying engineering for the first time. Most of these points cost little to agree on at the start and a lot to sort out later.

Ask every firm on your shortlist the same questions, and get the answers in writing.

## 1. Your own homework

- [ ] **1.1** The product's purpose, users, target cost, volumes, and markets are written down, even roughly.
- [ ] **1.2** You know which stage you are buying: a feasibility study, a working prototype, or a design ready for production.
  Each stage needs a different amount of work, and proposals are only comparable for the same stage.
- [ ] **1.3** The fixed constraints are listed: size, battery life, enclosure, certifications, launch date, and budget.
- [ ] **1.4** Someone on your side owns the project and can make decisions quickly.

## 2. Fit and experience

- [ ] **2.1** The firm has done work close to yours, such as similar radios, power sources, volumes, or markets.
- [ ] **2.2** The firm has the capacity to start when you need it and to keep your project staffed through the schedule, alongside its other work.
- [ ] **2.3** You have talked with the engineers who will do the work, not only the sales contact.
- [ ] **2.4** You know who does the work, whether staff, contractors, or a partner overseas, and who answers for it.
- [ ] **2.5** Past work is shown in whatever form its NDAs allow, or past clients are offered as references.

## 3. Scope and deliverables

- [ ] **3.1** The proposal lists the deliverables: requirements, schematics, layout, BOM, firmware source and build instructions, test plan, and a manufacturing package with programming files.
- [ ] **3.2** Design files are delivered in the native formats of the CAD tools used, with part libraries, stackup, and design rules, not only as PDFs and Gerbers.
  Without them, the next engineer has to redraw the design to change it.
- [ ] **3.3** What counts as done is written down for each phase, such as boards brought up and tested against the requirements.
- [ ] **3.4** Assumptions and exclusions are listed, such as enclosure design, certification testing, production test fixtures, and app or cloud software.

## 4. Ownership and confidentiality

- [ ] **4.1** The contract assigns you ownership of the designs, source code, and documents produced for you, and says when ownership passes, such as on payment.
- [ ] **4.2** Any of the firm's own reused designs or code is named, with a perpetual, irrevocable, royalty-free license that lets you build, sell, and change the product, and that passes to a buyer if your company is sold.
- [ ] **4.3** Third-party software in the firmware, including open-source code and vendor SDKs, is listed with its license terms.
  Some open-source licenses, such as the GPL, can require you to publish your own code if it is combined with theirs and shipped.
- [ ] **4.4** An NDA is in place before you share details, and it covers anyone the firm brings in.

## 5. Process and communication

- [ ] **5.1** The plan has review points where you see and approve the design before money is spent on boards or tooling.
- [ ] **5.2** Design reviews are part of the plan, ideally with at least one reviewer who did not do the design.
- [ ] **5.3** You get regular progress reports and access to work in progress, such as a shared repository.
- [ ] **5.4** Scope changes go through a written change process, with the cost and schedule shown before work starts.

## 6. Cost and schedule

- [ ] **6.1** The pricing model is clear, whether fixed price per phase, time and materials, or a retainer, and so is what happens when an estimate runs over.
- [ ] **6.2** You know which costs the price includes: prototype boards, parts, test equipment, lab fees, and certification.
- [ ] **6.3** The schedule shows lead times for boards and parts and the number of board spins planned.
  A plan with one spin and no slack leaves no room for the fixes bring-up usually turns up.
- [ ] **6.4** The contract says who pays to fix design errors found at bring-up, as distinct from changes you ask for.
- [ ] **6.5** If the project stops early, you receive the work done so far, in native formats, for what you have paid.

## 7. Toward production

- [ ] **7.1** The design is planned for the factory that will build it, with your contract manufacturer involved early.
- [ ] **7.2** Compliance is planned from the start: target markets, the radio approach, and pre-scans before formal testing.
- [ ] **7.3** The firm checks parts for lifecycle status, lead time, and second sources before layout.
- [ ] **7.4** Production testing is designed in, not added at the end.
- [ ] **7.5** Handover is planned: documentation, a walkthrough of the design, and the terms for support after the project ends.
